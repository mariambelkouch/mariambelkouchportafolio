01 — The thesis

Organizations are designed,
whether intentionally or not.

When organizations become more complex, the symptoms often surface as “People problems”: founders become bottlenecks, teams stop taking ownership, managers escalate decisions, and good people compensate for bad processes.

I look for the design problem underneath the symptom — then build the system that allows people to operate without compensating for it.

Complexity is inevitable.
Ambiguity is optional.

Fairness has a cost, and I've paid it.

At Hotel du Vin, tips had always gone to management. I moved them to the people working the shift — money leaving the hands of the people who'd approved it. I did it anyway.

Diagnosis comes before design.

At FIT 100, I spent weeks building HR policies, a handbook, automations — solving the problem the founders had described to me. Then I actually diagnosed it: interviewed the whole team, sat with each founder separately. They weren't aligned with each other — that was the real problem, not the missing paperwork. Everything else on this site came after we fixed that, not before.

Say the hard thing, especially when it's costly to say.

When Havona's funding round failed, six people had to leave. I designed the severance framework myself and worked with legal every step of the way, rather than letting a process run without me in the room. Zero legal challenges, no unbudgeted cost — though that was never really the point of doing it properly.

I didn't start my career in People.

I started in Bristol, managing a bar team.

At the time, I wasn't thinking about organizational design, people strategy, or operating models. I was thinking about very practical things: how to run a good shift, how to build a team people wanted to stay in, and how to make work feel fair.

I had worked for managers myself. I knew what it felt like when you were treated as a pair of hands rather than a person.

So when I became a manager, I tried something simple:

I treated my team the way I wished I had been treated.

I listened. I trusted them. I tried to make the rota fair. I gave people room to grow. I paid attention to the small things that made work harder than it needed to be.

And something happened.

People trusted me.

They stayed. They grew. They recommended people to join us. And eventually, the way we had designed the bar team became a model other parts of the business adopted.

That experience changed the way I thought about management.

I started to see that the environment around people shapes their behaviour just as much as the people themselves do.

That was my first lesson in organizational design. I just didn't have the language for it yet.

From People to systems.

Over the years, I worked at very different scales: from hospitality, to a 2,000-person international organization, to founder-led and remote companies where I was building People functions from scratch across 12 countries.

The context kept changing. But I kept seeing the same patterns.

A hiring problem that was really a problem with how work was designed.

A manager who had become a bottleneck because decision rights were unclear.

A compensation problem that was actually an incentive problem.

A team that appeared to lack ownership, when the system around them kept pulling decisions back to the centre.

And eventually I started asking a different question:

What if the problem isn't the person? What if it's the system we've asked them to operate in?

That question is at the centre of the work I'm doing now.

I became less interested in fixing individual People problems and more interested in understanding the systems producing them.

I'm interested in the space between how an organization is supposed to work and how work actually moves through it — through decisions, information, incentives, relationships, roles and informal networks.

I call that work organizational design.

Not the org-chart version of it.

The deeper version.

The design of the conditions in which people make decisions, coordinate, understand what matters, and ultimately do their work.

This is also why I started writing Organizations Are Designed.

Volume I is about learning to see what is actually happening inside an organization: the debt created by shortcuts, the backstage organization beneath the formal one, the gap between communication and shared understanding, the way systems overpower individual intent, and what happens when authority keeps gravitating back to the founder.

I'm trying to build a body of work around those patterns — and around a question I keep coming back to:

How do we design organizations with enough clarity that people don't need to rely on heroics, workarounds, or permission to make the organization work?

Because I don't think complexity is the problem.

Complexity is inevitable.
Ambiguity is optional.
